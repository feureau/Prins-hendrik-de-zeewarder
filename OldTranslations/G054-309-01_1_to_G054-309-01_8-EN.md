**Notes kept during my stay in Macassar from the 6th to the 10th of February 1837.**

**Monday, 6 February** – We had scarcely dropped anchor when a boat from the *Boreas* arrived, carrying the First Officer, Lieutenant 1st Class Baars, who presented Mr. Arriens with the program for my reception in Macassar. H.M. Corvette *Boreas* fired a salute of 21 guns, as did the colonial vessels, which we returned. No sooner had this been done than the fort fired a similar salute. Around 5:15 p.m., a commission boarded the ship to welcome me on behalf of Mr. Bousquet. It was agreed upon their departure that I would go ashore the following day at 8 o’clock. Nothing else of note occurred by the end of the day.

**Tuesday, 7 February** – The aforementioned commission arrived at 8:30 a.m. to escort me ashore, accompanying me to the landing. As I departed, the *Bellona* fired a 21-gun salute, followed by the *Boreas* and the colonial vessels. We rowed past the wooden pier, built in 1824 but collapsed during the last west monsoon due to lack of repairs.

Upon reaching the landing, I was received by the Resident of Macassar, who immediately accompanied me to his house. There, all the notables of the city were assembled. Mr. Bousquet introduced them to me: the Fiscal Council of Justice, members of the Orphan Chamber, and so on. Then came the Chinese Captain, the Chinese Lieutenant, the Malay chiefs, and others. After this, I was introduced to the officers and cadets of the *Boreas*, as well as the gentlemen of the garrison. Next, two envoys from the Sultan of Goa were brought in, having been sent upon hearing of my arrival. These two envoys were the Sultan’s father-in-law and a high-ranking official. They were richly dressed and followed by a small retinue of subordinates. After delivering a few words, they made a *slamet* (greeting) and departed. I shall skip a few unremarkable hours.

Thus began my visit. Nothing else of note occurred this day. At 5 p.m., we proceeded to Fort Rotterdam. The first thing I saw was the church—a rather charming, old, and somewhat warm building. After viewing it, we went to the residence of Diponegoro, our former enemy in Java and now our prisoner. It may be dangerous to lift the veil over this history, but I shall venture. Everyone knows that Diponegoro rebelled against us, and even his capture, in my view, remains a stain on old Dutch loyalty. It is true he was a rebel, but he came to end a war that had cost so many lives on both sides—and, more importantly, he came trusting in Dutch good faith to negotiate. It was then that he was seized on the orders of General De Kock. I believe this affair, which greatly served our interests (regarding our possession of all Java), has done us the greatest harm. For if, to our misfortune, we face more war in Java, one of two things will happen: either we or the Javanese will be destroyed, for no chief will ever again wish to have dealings with us. This will not only happen in Java but everywhere. I am convinced that the reason the Bonjol stronghold in Sumatra has not surrendered is nothing other than the Bonjolese chiefs saying, *"If I surrender or even negotiate with the Dutch, I will surely be dishonored like Diponegoro."*

But enough of this. Upon arriving at Diponegoro’s residence, he immediately came to meet me. After shaking my hand, he asked if I wished to see his upper quarters, as he was receiving us below—a thing he never does. Upon my assent, he took my right arm and led me up the stairs. In his upper room, he told Mr. Bousquet how pleased he was that we had come to see his miserable dwelling. Now, it is certain that for a former ruler, two small rooms—perhaps eight feet square—are very little. Yet he seemed to bear his fate with great patience and was quite cheerful. I was told he spends most of his time copying the Quran and drawing.

His captivity has been somewhat eased: he is allowed to walk within the fort during the day whenever he wishes and may go for rides, but only with the Governor’s prior knowledge, accompanied by the duty officer and two hussars. However, he rarely makes use of this permission.

His pride has much diminished. Formerly, he spoke only Javanese, but later, when he began speaking Malay, he always addressed Europeans in the familiar *jouw*—a term used only with native servants, which Europeans consider an insult.

After spending some time with Diponegoro, he led me back downstairs, and we parted at the door with a *Tabe Toewan* (Good day, sir). From there, we toured the ramparts, seeing the arsenal and barracks. Everything seemed, like the fort itself, not very sturdy, according to the commandant, who told me he was convinced that if the guns on the walls were fired, the walls would collapse. This is no wonder, for these walls have stood as they always have, and beneath them once ran a road, now filled in and said to be the dwelling place of all manner of creatures—iguanas, crabs, and the like—which, I was told, have undermined the foundations, though I saw no cracks. In the barracks, an old warehouse now repurposed, we saw a company of Bugis volunteers (or so they were called), soon to be sent to Java. Once there, they would surely never see their homeland again. The last thing we saw in Fort Rotterdam was the old town hall, a very sturdy building from the time of the Company, now serving as the artillery barracks.

On our way out, I noticed under a vault behind the church several pieces of artillery. When I asked what they were, I was told that the rear part of the church had been added later and was used as a powder magazine. It was then full of gunpowder, and there was no lightning rod. How dangerous! If this powder were to explode, not only would the city’s only church be destroyed, but I believe much of the fort’s other buildings would go with it.

Having stepped into the carriage, we drove to the Governor’s house, where we spent the rest of the evening.

**Wednesday, 8 February** – Having risen at 5 a.m., I made, in the company of Messrs. Bousquet, Arriens, Van Lawick de Braauw, Michielsen, and Maxwell (who was assigned to me), a very pleasant horseback ride through the environs of Macassar. The road was most agreeable—fresh, winding between beautiful green rice fields, past the house of a Celebes queen, along the seashore, and back into the city. On the beach stood a multitude of native vessels, including *paduakhans*. These little boats are nailed together with wooden pegs, always with small beams, and though poorly constructed, however small they may be, the inhabitants of the neighboring lands use them for trade, sailing from Macassar to Bali (approximately [blank] miles) and from Riouw to Buton (approximately [blank] miles).

The land around Macassar is rather marshy, largely due to the rice fields, which must always be flooded. Rice is almost the sole sustenance of the natives in most of the lands around our possessions. The rice is first sown by the men in small mounds, as is done in Holland with peas. When it reaches half a hand’s breadth in height, the women and children transplant each seedling at regular intervals.
